\section{Introduction}
As mentioned in the previous chapter, classical simulation of quantum circuits provides an essential tool 
for verification and validation, despite its inherent limitations. 
This chapter delves into the methods and advancements in classical simulation, 
particularly focusing on stabilizer decomposition and the role of magic states in extending the capabilities of these simulations.
Classical simulation methods that store a complete description of an $n$-qubit quantum state 
as a complex vector of size $2^n$ are effective only for a small number of qubits, typically around 30. 
For example, Shor's factoring algorithm has been simulated with 31 qubits and approximately half a million gates (Paragraph 1). 
However, for certain classes of quantum circuits, such as those composed of gates from the Clifford group, 
classical simulation can be significantly more efficient. 
This is known as the \textit{Gottesman-Knill theorem}.

\begin{theorem}[Gottesman-Knill Theorem]
    \label{thm:gottesman-knill}
    A Clifford computation can be efficiently classically simulated.
\end{theorem}

Building on the foundational concepts discussed in the previous chapter, 
where we explored the graphical representation of quantum circuits using ZX-Calculus, 
we now turn to the specific techniques for simulating these circuits classically. 
ZX-Calculus not only aids in visualizing quantum operations 
but also simplifies the process of identifying and manipulating stabilizer states, 
which are central to many classical simulation methods (Paragraph 4).

Stabilizer circuits are particularly notable for their applications in quantum error correction. 
These circuits are initialized in stabilizer states, evolve under stabilizer operations, 
and are measured in the Pauli basis. 
Classical simulation of these circuits is efficient due to their restriction to the Clifford group operations (Paragraph 3). 
However, to achieve universal quantum computation, we must incorporate non-Clifford elements, such as magic states. 
The introduction of magic states, which extend the computational capabilities of stabilizer circuits, 
brings an exponential cost to the simulation. 
Nonetheless, when circuits are predominantly composed of Clifford gates, classical simulation remains feasible (Paragraph 3).
