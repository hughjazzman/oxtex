\section{Benchmarking}
To determine the effectiveness of our heuristic methods thus far,
we need to benchmark them against known methods.
In this chapter, we introduce the various benchmarking methods we use to evaluate our heuristics.
This is based off of various previous work, particularly those using ZX-calculus 
\cite{Kissinger2022cat,Kissinger_2022,sutcliffe2024proccut,Codsi2022}.
We use QuiZX \cite{quizx}, with features added from \cite{Kissinger2022cat},
especially the \catstate{n} decompositions.
QuiZX itself was a Rust port of PyZX, originally built in Python \cite{kissinger2020Pyzx}.

\subsection{Random Clifford+T}
Random Clifford+T circuits coming from \textit{exponentiated Pauli unitaries} 
were used in the benchmark of the BSS decomposition \cite{Kissinger2020}, 
and subsequently continued use in the benchmark of the \catstate{n} decomposition \cite{Kissinger2022cat}.
The operators in this form naturally arise in the literature, 
including Clifford+T circuit representations \cite{gosset2014}, as cited in \cite{Kissinger2020}.
We introduce their definitions, pertaining to the ZX-calculus.

\begin{definition}[Unitary]
    A unitary $U$ is a map of the form
\begin{equation}
    \tikzfig{unitary}
\end{equation} 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Pauli exponential \cite{Kissinger2024}]
A \textit{Pauli exponential} is any unitary of the form
\begin{equation}
    U := e^{\pm i\theta P_1 \otimes \dots\otimes P_n}
\end{equation}
where $P_i$ are Paulis, and $\theta\in \mathbb{R}$ is a phase, and the exponential is defined as
\begin{equation}
    e^A := \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{A^k}{k!}
\end{equation}
for any map $A$, where $A^k$ is
\begin{equation}
    \cbox{\boxmap{A^k}} := \underbrace{\tikzfig{expmapa}}_k
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[\cite{Kissinger2024}]
    Let $\vec{P} = P_1 \otimes \dots \otimes P_n$ be any Pauli string with no trivial entries, 
    so $\forall i, P_i \in \{X,Y,Z\}$.
    Then the Pauli exponential $e^{i\theta \vec{P}}$ is a ZX diagram where
\begin{equation}
    e^{\pm i\theta \vec{P}} \approx \tikzfig{pauli_exp}
\end{equation}
Here $U_j\in \{\cbox{\idwire}, \cbox{\hadamard}, \cbox{\redphase{\frac{\pi}{2}}}\}$ such that
\begin{equation}
    \tikzfig{upuj} = \cbox{\greenphase{\pi}}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Choosing $\theta = (2k+1)\frac{\pi}{4}$ for $k\in \mathbb{Z}$, we can generate a random Clifford+T circuit
with a fixed depth by composing these exponentiated Pauli unitaries.
This fixes the $T$-count of the circuit, and can be used to benchmark decompositions.

\subsection{Random IQP}
\label{sec:random-iqp}
Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial (IQP) circuits were used in the benchmark of \cite{Codsi2022}.
These circuits were chosen as a possible model to demonstrate quantum supremacy \cite{Bremner_2017}.
They can likely be built in a quantum computer, while at the same time,
they are a class of commuting quantum computations which are shown to be hard to simulate classically,
even when adding in the presence of physically motivated constraints, 
such as sparsity and noise \cite{Bremner_2017}.
While it is noted that IQP circuits have been questioned in their ability 
to demonstrate quantum supremacy \cite{codsi2023,Codsi2022},
we show that the heuristic approach gives performance that match previous work.

First, we introduce the CZ-gate.

\begin{definition}[CZ-gate]
    The CZ-gate is the controlled Z-gate, defined as
\begin{equation}
    CZ := \tikzfig{cz}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

More generally, we can define the controlled Z($\alpha$)-gate, 
where $\alpha=k\frac{\pi}{2}, k\in \mathbb{Z}$, so $CZ(\pi) = CZ$.

\begin{definition}[$CZ(\alpha)$-gate \cite{coecke2017picturing}]
    The controlled Z($\alpha$)-gate is the phase gadget,
\begin{equation}
    CZ(\alpha) := \tikzfig{cza}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}


\begin{definition}[IQP circuit \cite{Bremner_2016,codsi2023}]
    An IQP circuit is a circuit composed of Hadamard gates, $CZ(\frac{k\pi}{2})$-gates,
    and $T^m$-gates, where $k,m\in \mathbb{Z}$.
    Thus, the following \autoref{fig:def-IQP} is a simplified IQP circuit.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \ctikzfig{def-IQP}
    \caption{An IQP circuit as a ZX-diagram, for $x_i,y_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}$, 
    as defined in \cite{codsi2023}}
    \label{fig:def-IQP}
\end{figure}
\end{definition}

Intuitively, the commutative nature of the phase gadgets can be seen directly in the ZX-calculus.
We also see the presence of many phase gadgets which only have 2 legs,
which lends itself directly to the strength of our heuristic (\ref{eq:cut-heuristic-combined}),
considering the number of \catstate{3} states (\ref{eq:cut-heuristic-cat3}).

In \cite{codsi2023}, dense IQP circuits were found to be simulable in $O(\frac{\log^2 n}{n}2^{n})$ time,
where $n$ is the number of qubits,
where dense IQP circuits are those with $O(n^2)$ phase gadgets, and thus $t= O(n^2)$ $T$-count.
This is faster than $O(2^n/poly(n))$ for any $poly(n)$,
compared to $O(n^4 2^{O(n^2)})$ of just using stabiliser decomposition.
They concluded that current hardware is probably not enough to demonstrate quantum supremacy
using IQP circuits.
We note, however, that the heuristic approach can easily give us a better upper bound than than the general one.

\begin{lemma}
    Given a $n$-qubit IQP circuit, the heuristic approach to stabiliser decomposition
    will give $O(2^n)$ terms.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Note that as stated in \cite{codsi2023}, we can decompose the IQP circuit into $O(2^n)$ terms
    just by cutting the $T$-like spiders corresponding to the $n$ qubits.
    It suffices to show that the heuristic will pick the correct cut decompositions,
    or result in an equivalent number of terms.

    First, note that we can set the algorithm \ref{alg:stabiliser-decomposition} 
    to skip the \nameref{alg:zx-simplify} step between each cut.
    Consider a $T$-like spider connected to a leg of a phase gadget.
    Note that there are only $n$ of such spiders.
    We know it will always be connected to $k \geq 1$ phase gadgets 
    (i.e. the $x_i$ $T$-like spiders in \autoref{fig:def-IQP}).
    Also, the $T$-like spiders in the phase gadgets themselves are only connected 
    to a single phase gadget by definition (i.e. the $y_{i,j}$ $T$-like spiders in \autoref{fig:def-IQP}).
    If $k=1$ for both legs of the phase gadget, it doesn't matter which $T$-like spider we pick, 
    as they would be equivalent cuts.
    Otherwise, if $k > 1$ for some leg, the heuristic will always pick that $T$-like spider, 
    and since its $\alpha_h \leq 0.2 < 0.25$
    is better than any of the \catstate{n} decompositions, 
    it will be picked over them in the greedy algorithm.

    If we are left with $T$-like spiders where all are connected to $k=1$ phase gadgets,
    then since the only cat state present is the \catstate{3},
    its decomposition of 2 terms will give an equivalent number of terms as the cut decomposition.
    Thus, the heuristic will always pick the correct cuts, 
    or use the \catstate{3} decomposition, resulting in an equivalent number of terms.
\end{proof}

While this is not as good as the $O(\frac{\log^2 n}{n}2^{n})$ time of the dense IQP circuits,
it gives a much closer upper bound than the general bound of $O(n^4 2^{O(n^2)})$, 
and is relatively easy to implement in practice.
In fact, we can generalise this even further.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:reduced-gadget-cut}
    Suppose we have ZX-diagram in reduced gadget form, 
    which has $n$ $T$-like spiders not part of phase gadgets,
    and $\tilde{O}(n^k)$
    \footnote{$\tilde{O}(f(n)) = O(f(n) \log^p f(n))$ for some $p$ is a convenient shorthand 
    ignoring polylogarithmic factors} 
    phase gadgets, for $k\in \mathbb{R}_+$.
    Then the stabiliser decomposition can be completed in $O(2^n)$ time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Simply cut the $n$ $T$-like spiders to get $O(2^n)$ terms.
    Since the phase gadgets will now be legless, the diagram is easy to simulate as scalars.
\end{proof}

We have that for dense IQP circuits, $k=2$.

The resulting efficiency of just using cuts is then
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \frac{n}{n+\tilde{O}(n^k)} = \frac{1}{1+\tilde{O}(n^{k-1})}
\end{equation}
It can be noted that for $k \leq 1$, the cut approach will not really be useful,
since $n^{k-1} \leq 1$ so its $\alpha \geq 0.5$ as $n\to\infty$.
However, for $k > 1$, we see that $\alpha < 0.5$ as $n\to\infty$ is inversely correlated with $n^{k-1} > 1$,
and so it might be useful to use a different metric that is not based on the $T$-count.
Instead, as observed in \cite{codsi2023}, we see that the growth of the number of terms
is more like $O(2^{\beta n})$ for some $0 < \beta \leq 1$.
For dense IQP circuits, they obtained $\beta \approx 0.93$.
For sparse IQP circuits where $k=1$ (so there were $O(n\log n)$ phase gadgets), 
they obtained $0.5 < \beta < 0.93$.
Compare this to using $\alpha$ where we have the growth being 
\begin{equation}
O(2^{\alpha \cdot \tilde{O}(n^k)}) = O\left(2^{\frac{\tilde{O}(n^k)}{\tilde{O}(n^{k-1})}}\right) 
= O(2^{O(n)})
\end{equation}
which ends up being equivalent.

\begin{definition}[$\beta$ efficiency]
    \label{def:beta-efficiency}
    Suppose we have ZX-diagram in reduced gadget form, 
    which has $n$ $T$-like spiders not part of phase gadgets,
    and $\tilde{O}(n^k)$ phase gadgets, for $k\in \mathbb{R}_{> 1}$.
    Let $p$ be the number of terms in the stabiliser decomposition $D$.
    We define the $\beta$ \textit{efficiency} as
    \begin{equation}
        \beta(D) = \frac{p}{n}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

We note that the cut heuristic as defined in (\ref{eq:cut-heuristic-combined})
works very well due to the presence of many \catstate{3}.
In the general case where this may not be true, 
the conceptualisation of different heuristics could be developed to
exploit any classes of circuits that have a structure such that 
its reduced gadget form allows for Proposition \ref{prop:reduced-gadget-cut} to be applied.

\subsection{Modified Hidden Shift}
\label{sec:modified-hidden-shift}

Hidden shift circuits have been used in various benchmarks since being introduced in \cite{Bravyi_2016bss},
starting from the benchmark of the BSS decomposition, 
and continuing to the benchmark of the \catstate{n} decomposition 
\cite{Bravyi_2019,Kissinger_2022,Kissinger2022cat,Peres_2023}.
However, it was noted that improvements to QuiZX trivialised the decomposition of these circuits,
with a conjecture of the class of circuits being simulable in polynomial time \cite{Codsi2022}.
Thus, in \cite{koch2023}, they introduced the \textit{modified hidden shift} circuits.

First, we note that the $T$-count of the hidden shift circuits only comes from
the $T$-like spiders appearing in CCZ gates in the circuits.
We introduce the definition of the CCZ gate, shown as presented in \cite{Kissinger_2022}.

\begin{definition}[CCZ gate \cite{Kissinger_2022}]
    The CCZ gate is the controlled-CZ-gate, defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:7t-ccz}
\tikzfig{ccz-box} := \tikzfig{ccz} \ =\ 
\sqrt{2}^5\ 
\tikzfig{7t-ccz}
\end{equation}
It arises from simplifying the `textbook' presentation of CCZ or Toffoli in terms of CNOT and T gates 
(see e.g.~\cite{Nielsen_Chuang_2010}, Section 4.3). 
The 4-spider version is the following:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:4t-ccz}
\tikzfig{ccz} \ =\ 
4e^{i\frac\pi4}\ 
\tikzfig{4t-ccz}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

While the structure of the original hidden shift circuits made use of CCZ gates,
the modified hidden shift circuits used controlled swap gates instead.
The simple addition of the CNOT and Hadamard gates to form the new controlled swap gate

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:log-time-improvement}
    Suppose we have a circuit in reduced gadget form with a $T$-count $t$.
    Given $\alpha_h < \alpha$ using the heuristics (\ref{eq:cut-heuristic-combined}, 
    \ref{eq:cut-heuristic-pair}) compared against the default \texttt{quizx} implementation,
    the log time improvement is $(\alpha - \alpha_h)t - \log t $.
    The overhead of computing the heuristic can be given by $\log t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Without the heuristic, we have that the time taken is $O(t^2 2^{\alpha t})$.
    With the heuristic, we have that the time taken is $O(t^3 2^{\alpha_h t})$.
    We assume that the constant factors are comparable.
    Taking the log of the ratio of the 2 terms, the log time improvement is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \log\left(\frac{t^2 2^{\alpha t}}{t^3 2^{\alpha_h t}}\right)
    &= -\log t + \log 2^{\alpha t} - \log 2^{\alpha_h t}\\
    &= -\log t + \alpha t - \alpha_h t\\
    &= (\alpha - \alpha_h)t - \log t 
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

We can see that the overhead can be significant for low $t$ where $(\alpha - \alpha_h)t < \log t$, 
but since $\log t = o((\alpha - \alpha_h)t)$\footnote{using little-o notation} for any $\alpha > \alpha_h$, 
the overhead becomes less significant as $t$ increases.
For the results shown here, we are limited to showing low $T$-counts as the time taken 
to run experiments is not feasible for higher $T$-counts.
In \autoref{fig:count8-50-pauli}, \ref{fig:count100-pauli}, 
we see that the number of completed simulations before timeout
is lower for the heuristic for $q \leq 50$, but $q=100$ has a higher number of completed simulations.
We expect that if we run this experiment with a much longer timeout,
then the heuristic will show its superiority in terms of time to match that of its improved $\alpha$.
We can quantify the expected $T$-counts for which this occurs by solving for $(\alpha - \alpha_h)t > \log t$,
shown in the appendix \autoref{sec:app-results}.
